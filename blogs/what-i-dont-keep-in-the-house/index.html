What I Don't Keep in the House

People approve most AI permission prompts without reading them. I've ended up deciding ahead of time instead, both with Claude at my desk and with food at home, and at my mother's table that still wasn't enough.
October 11, 2026 · 5 min · 905 words · Nachiket Namjoshi

93 Percent#

People approved Claude Code’s permission prompts roughly 93% of the time. Anthropic shared that number to make a point: the more prompts you see, the less you read them, and eventually the human check stops being much of a check.

Anthropic describes two fixes. One is to let another system make the routine calls: auto mode, where a classifier decides which actions are safe enough to approve. The other is to limit what the agent can do in the first place, so some actions aren’t possible at all. Either way, the idea is to stop relying on someone paying attention to every single action.

I read that and recognised myself, at my desk and around food.

Checking After the Fact#

Most of my “yes” clicks these days are to Claude, allowing it to run whatever command it wants for what feels like the hundredth time. I rarely go back to check what it actually ran. I’d like to think I’d notice if something went wrong.

I track my calories and macros, and I’m fairly conscious of what I eat. But plenty of days I don’t track anything until after I’ve eaten. I’m tired, I’m out somewhere, or I just don’t feel like it. I fill in the numbers later.

Both still give me the feeling of being in control. But I’ve started paying less attention to both.

Deciding Ahead#

What has worked better is deciding ahead of time.

I got bored of approving things and switched on auto mode. Now the classifier decides which commands are safe enough to run without asking me. It works well enough that I barely think about permissions anymore. I didn’t get better at paying attention. I handed the job to another system, one that Anthropic itself says will sometimes miss.

At home, my wife and I do the shopping ourselves and don’t keep much junk food around. Most of what we buy is protein sources and the food we normally eat. If I feel like eating something I shouldn’t late at night, there’s usually nothing there. I don’t have to win an argument with myself. The option just isn’t there.

Going out is different. I can’t empty a restaurant’s kitchen. So when I know there’ll be pizza or noodles with family or friends, I drink two scoops of protein shake before I go, so I don’t arrive hungry. It doesn’t make anything impossible. It just means I’m not at my weakest when the food arrives.

These decisions cost something too. Claude still interrupts me for permission on things I couldn’t care less about. And when guests come over, there isn’t much in the house to offer them, only whatever other people brought us. So I either make an acceptable version of a dish in advance or order something just for that visit, making sure not much is left over. A wall around my eating ends up as a wall around my hospitality.

What the Classifier Can’t Hear#

Claude has sent messages on GChat in auto mode without asking me first. I’d approved sending messages plenty of times before, but only after I’d read them. This time I didn’t see them until they were sent. The meaning was right. The tone wasn’t. It wasn’t how I would have said it.

As far as the classifier was concerned, sending the message was safe enough. It hadn’t checked whether it sounded like me. I’d written before about how many corrections it took before my AI code reviewer sounded like me. The classifier didn’t even know tone was something I cared about.

I scolded Claude for going rogue. Then I set a rule: messages need my approval before they go. Only the second one fixed anything. A line often attributed to Deming says “a bad system beats a good person every time.”

At the Table#

The last time I visited my mother, during the festival season, she’d made sweets. I said no. She was disappointed. It felt like her effort had been wasted.

No system decided this for me. I knew exactly whose sweets they were. I thought about eating them, and I did give in a little. I ate a bit and stopped.

Afterwards I felt bad. Not guilty about eating a little, not guilty about refusing either, and not proud of sticking to my diet. Just bad.

She still makes passing comments about not being sure I’ll eat her food enough to “justify” her effort.

Another time, at a restaurant, having decided to eat healthier, I spent so long going through the menu on my phone that it looked like I was more interested in my phone than in the people I was with.

The Quiet Moments#

None of this covers the moments I actually slip. Most of the time I know perfectly well what I’m supposed to eat. I just want the food anyway, after a long day or in a quiet moment when the temptation feels stronger than whatever reason I had for avoiding it.

I still think building restrictions in is useful wherever I can manage it. Some mistakes even turn into a rule after they happen once, like the GChat messages. I could make an exception for my mother’s festival sweets too. But that wouldn’t tell me what to do when I’m sitting at her table.

At her table I could see exactly what mattered, and I still don’t know if I got it right.
