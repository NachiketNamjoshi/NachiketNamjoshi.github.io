To Get Back to the Point

A chatbot knew everything about my eye prescription but kept dragging me toward a retina test. Almost every line in my AI instructions pushes back on habits like that, and I've never once checked whether a newer model still needs them.
November 1, 2026 · 7 min · 1319 words · Nachiket Namjoshi

The Eye Prescription#

This week I asked a chatbot, without my custom instructions, to explain my eye prescription and all the lens and glasses jargon. I mentioned why I thought it needed checking again: things looked blurry, I had a mild headache, a few other things.

It held on to the headache. It kept asking me about it, one question after another, like it was interrogating me. When I asked about eye tests, it kept pushing me towards a dilated retina test. Maybe some of that was a reasonable precaution. But it kept coming back to it even after I’d explained what I actually wanted.

In the end I had to tell it: there’s no significant health issue here, we’re beating around the bush, get back to the point.

It did, and it did it well. The model knew the answer. That was never the problem. The problem was getting it to hand the answer over.

What My Instructions Actually Do#

My usual setup instructions contain:

If you ever feel the need to blabber on, stop. Do not waste my time by trying to investigate tangents which may not be directly related to subject at hand just to keep me on the app and increase user engagement.

The rest of the list is the same kind of thing. Don’t agree with me just to be agreeable. No flattery, no “great question”. If you’re not sure, say so. Push back. Find the weak and blind spots in my thinking, even if I didn’t ask.

That last one might sound like I’m asking for tangents. I’m not. Flag the headache once, tell me why it might matter, then get back to my question. That’s pointing out a blind spot. Coming back to it again and again after I’ve said it’s not the issue isn’t. That’s the model steering the conversation where it wants to go.

When I look at the list, hardly any of it asks the model to be smarter or to know more. Almost every line is telling it to stop doing something it does by default.

Where That Line Came From#

I use ChatGPT for random stuff I get curious about, mostly so I don’t burn through my Claude limits. Claude Code is for everything in the terminal. I treat them the same.

After a while I started seeing more and more “we should do this next” at the end of answers on ChatGPT. It got pushier. Then one day it clicked that this was on purpose. I can’t prove that. But it’s what it felt like, and nothing since has changed my mind.

Opus does exactly the same. Going from Sonnet to Opus made things better overall, no question. But the tangents went up. “While I was going through this, I found this.” “It would be best if we fix that as well.” Then I have to give it more context on why what it found is fine and not a priority right now. If I ignore the suggestions, they come back in the same session, stronger each time, until they get on my nerves. Even after Claude Code compacts the conversation to free up room, they’re still there.

So the model got smarter and, in this one way, more annoying to use. That line in my instructions is there because of it.

100 to 50-50#

When I started using these tools, I’d have said the results were 100% the model, because I didn’t know better back then. Since then I’ve kept tweaking instructions, config, MCPs. Now I’d call it about 50-50. I can’t measure that, and it’s not about intelligence or accuracy. It’s how much the experience depends on the model versus everything I’ve done to make it usable.

Other people see big gaps too. In one test, the same model on the same questions scored 93% in one setup and 77% in another. That gap came from code, how results were fed in and how tools were called, not from plain-English instructions like mine. But it shows how much the software around a model can change what you get out of it, even when the model stays the same.

My half didn’t grow because I made the model more capable. Most of it grew out of the fight, and some of it is just me learning to tell the model what I want. It has a limit too: Haiku can’t give me what Opus does, no matter what I put in the config. The way I see it, the model sets what’s possible, and my setup is like unlocking easy mode: getting it to do those things without a fight.

Building on Sand#

The usual pushback is that all this setup is a workaround for today’s model. The next one comes along and you don’t need it anymore, so piling it up is building on sand.

That has not happened to me with AI. It has with other things. I’ve written utility scripts for all sorts of stuff I use. I once wrote a script to control Spotify. Then one fine day, Spotify started working with the native music controls itself. My script became pointless, so I dropped it.

But here’s the honest part: I’ve never deleted or even toned down a line in my AI instructions. I never felt the need to. And when a new model came out, I never tried it without them to check. So I can’t really say the sand argument is wrong. I’ve just never tested it.

My guess is it wouldn’t make much difference. The agreeing, at least, comes from how these models are trained. Anthropic’s own researchers found that people rate answers that agree with them higher, so training on those ratings teaches the model to agree. My instructions are there to push back on that. They don’t change how the model was trained.

The Other Side#

To be fair, makers do fix some of this. In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update because it had become “overly flattering or agreeable”. They said they’d “focused too much on short-term feedback”, the thumbs up and thumbs down. When it gets bad enough, they pull it.

And the engagement suspicion has a fair counter. In a post about sensitive conversations, OpenAI mentions adding reminders to take breaks during long sessions. That doesn’t sound like a company trying to keep me on the app.

Where I’ve Landed#

I still don’t buy that the steering is harmless. If the headache had been a real worry, I’d have asked about it, or gone to a doctor first. The model had no reason to drag the conversation there. Whatever the reason behind it, I ended up somewhere other than where I needed to be, and I spent my time getting back.

And look at what OpenAI promised after the rollback: “more personalization features, giving users greater control over how ChatGPT behaves.” Maybe that means they want it fixed. But the fix still lands on me, writing more instructions.

So that’s where I am. The model does the knowing, and it does it better with every upgrade. My instructions do the steering, and a lot of that steering is pulling it back from its defaults.

Not all of it, though. “Push back on me” and “say when you’re not sure” aren’t fixing anything broken. A model that never flattered anyone still wouldn’t know I want to be argued with. Those lines are just me telling it who I am. My desktop setup is the same: it doesn’t make the tools better in general, it makes them better for me.

I’ll keep writing those lines. I don’t mind. Maintenance was always the deal, same as with my desktop scripts.

What I can’t work out is the mix. When my half keeps growing, how much of it is undoing what the model’s maker wants, and how much is just telling it who I am?
